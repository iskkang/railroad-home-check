How Many Trains Pass Near My House?

How to use FRA crossing records to estimate rail activity near a U.S. address without relying on guesswork or a railfan timetable.

Illustrative AI-generated landscape · not a photo of the searched property

There usually is not a public live timetable for freight trains

Passenger trains publish schedules. Freight railroads generally do not provide a consumer timetable showing every train that will pass a particular house. That is why homebuyers often struggle to answer a seemingly simple question: how active are these tracks?

FRA crossing inventory data can provide a useful proxy

The Federal Railroad Administration maintains grade-crossing inventory records that can include reported train movements. For a nearby crossing, the record may show daytime thru trains, nighttime thru trains, weekly train movements, and the year of the train-count information.

Why the count is not the same as a guarantee

Rail operations change. Seasonal traffic, rerouting, construction, railroad business conditions, and local switching can all affect what you actually hear. The reported count is best used as a due-diligence signal: a line with meaningful nighttime activity deserves an on-site visit at night.

Use distance and crossing location together

Two homes the same distance from a rail line can have very different experiences. A nearby public crossing can mean horn activity, while a home farther from the crossing may mainly experience rolling noise and vibration. Look at both the nearest crossing and reported train movement.

Important: FRA train-count fields are reported inventory estimates, not live schedules. Noise and vibration at a specific property depend on local conditions and should be verified on site.

Find the reported count in four steps

  1. Search the full address and confirm the map pin.
  2. Open Sound, then Rail lines & crossing records below the map.
  3. Record day and night thru-train estimates, crossing ID and count year.
  4. Inspect nearby crossings on the map and plan a visit at relevant hours.

Read a train-count example correctly

Illustration only: a crossing showing 12 day trains, 4 night trains and a 2024 count year reports an estimated operating pattern for that record. It does not mean four trains will pass tonight, or that each train follows the same schedule. The day and night periods in this report are 6 AM–6 PM and 6 PM–6 AM.

Do not add counts from neighboring crossings

One train can travel through several crossings. Adding every nearby record can count that train repeatedly. A crossing on another branch can describe a different route; the nearest crossing is a starting point, not the total traffic for every track around the home.

What if no crossing is returned?

The checker searches open, at-grade records within approximately 3.25 miles. A rail line can exist without a returned crossing. Review mapped rail geometry separately; a missing count or a missing record is unknown information, not zero trains.
