How to Check Flood Risk Before Buying a House

Start here

Confirm the address location on the official FEMA map, read the zone and map information, then investigate drainage, past water entry and insurance separately. Missing data does not mean low risk.

1. Confirm the building location

Check that the map pin is on the intended property. A street match or an approximate geocoder result may fall on the wrong side of a boundary. Use the official FEMA Map Service Center to review the location and relevant map information.

Our report is a preliminary public-data lookup, not a parcel survey or an insurance determination. If the result is unavailable, keep it unresolved and use the official map.

2. Read the result without overinterpreting it

FEMA uses different zones to describe mapped flood hazards. Being outside a mapped high-risk area does not eliminate flood risk. Read the full zone description and legend; do not infer the detailed meaning from a letter alone.

The NFIP explains that flood losses also occur outside high-risk areas. Treat the map as one source rather than a guarantee about a particular building.

3. Investigate the property itself

At a viewing, note ground slope, low entrances, below-grade rooms, drains and visible moisture or repair marks. Ask about previous water entry, drainage work and any supporting records. Have an appropriate professional investigate concerns rather than diagnosing the cause from appearance alone.

For example, a favorable map result and a damp basement are separate observations. The map should not cancel out the need to investigate the basement.

4. Ask the right follow-up questions

Ask the insurance provider about the coverage available for the specific property and situation. Bring the location and official map information to that discussion. Ask the relevant local authority about drainage information or records that would help resolve a specific concern.

Record the source, date and answer. Refresh the address report when returning to a saved home; its saved snapshot is not a live monitoring service.

5. Compare unresolved issues, too

Keep map results, visit observations and unanswered questions in separate columns. Two homes with the same map label can have different building conditions and unanswered questions. Avoid reducing the decision to a single color or score.
