Which U.S. cities are loudest? Road, rail and airplane noise where people actually live

Every city has a highway, a rail line or an airport. What differs is how many people live next to them. We matched the federal transportation noise map to where residents of 299 cities live, and the spread is wider than you might expect.

By the Home Surroundings Check editorial team · Drafted with AI assistance; figures checked against federal records

The short answer

In the median city we cover, about 2.9% of residents live where roads, railroads and aircraft add up to a modeled 24-hour average of 55 dB or more outdoors. In 24 cities it is 10% or more, led by Newark at 20.5%. In 30 cities it is under half a percent.

In 1974 EPA identified 55 dB outdoors as the level that prevents annoyance and interference with everyday activities such as conversation and sleep. For a sense of scale, the Bureau of Transportation Statistics compares 50 dB to a humming refrigerator. These are averages over a full day: a single freight train, horn or jet overhead is far louder while it passes.

Two kinds of places top the list. Old industrial cores such as Newark, Chicago and Hammond have rail yards, freight mainlines and interstates running through dense neighborhoods. Others are mid-size cities with a busy airport or air base close to housing: North Charleston, Wichita Falls, Laredo and Fresno all get more of their total from aircraft than from roads.

Size alone doesn't decide it. Phoenix and Austin are among the largest cities on the list and have under 2% of residents in 55 dB areas,. Seattle, Louisville and Fresno have smaller populations and roughly eight to ten times the share.

Which source matters where

Rail. Hammond, IN (7.7%), New Braunfels, TX (7.4%), Chicago, IL (7.1%), Bloomington, IN (6.7%), Newark, NJ (5.9%). In these cities busy rail lines run through residential neighborhoods rather than around them.

Aircraft. North Charleston, SC (13.9%), Wichita Falls, TX (12.3%), Miami, FL (11.3%), Laredo, TX (11.2%), Fresno, CA (10.7%). Each has a commercial airport or military airfield close to residential areas.

Roads. South Gate, CA (14.3%), Portland, ME (11.6%), Kissimmee, FL (11.0%), Salem, OR (8.9%), Seattle, WA (8.1%). Here interstates or state highways pass close to dense housing.

What this means for a home search

A city-wide share tells you how common loud blocks are, not whether a given house is on one. Even in Newark, four in five residents live below 55 dB, and every quiet city has homes backing onto a freeway. Three checks narrow it down:

  • Look up the address. Our report reads the same noise map at the property and shows road, rail and aircraft separately, next to the nearest crossing's train counts and the busiest nearby road.
  • Visit at the loud hours. Rush hour for roads, late evening for freight trains, and the arrival or departure pattern for airports, which can shift with the wind.
  • Check what blocks the sound. A wall, a row of buildings or a berm between the home and the source can matter more than distance. Ask about window ratings, since closed modern windows cut outdoor noise substantially.

How we measured it

We used USDOT's National Transportation Noise Map, which models road, rail and aviation noise across the country on a 30-meter grid as a 24-hour average (LAeq), from 2020 transportation data. For each city we took the 2020 census city limits and every census block group inside them, placed each block group at its population center, and read the modeled noise band there. The share is weighted by population. Where the combined layer showed a lower band than one of its own sources, we used the louder value.

This is a coarse, national model. BTS says it is meant for tracking trends, not for judging an individual location, and a block group's population center stands in for everyone who lives in it. Savannah, Georgia is left out because its center point did not fall inside the city limits. Treat the ranking as a guide to where loud blocks are common, then check the specific home.
