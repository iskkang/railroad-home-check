California's atmospheric river: one storm can bring a fifth of a year's rain. Here is how FEMA rates the flood risk

An atmospheric river reaches the California coast this week, with flash flood risk forecast for the Bay Area on Wednesday and the Los Angeles area on Thursday. We checked the 50 California cities we track against FEMA's flood ratings and NOAA rainfall normals: where river flood risk rates highest, what a 3-inch storm means in a city that averages 12 to 19 inches a year, and what to check in a home before you buy.

By the Home Surroundings Check editorial team · Drafted with AI assistance; figures checked against federal records

What happened

After a weekend of high surf and strong wind along the West Coast, another storm arrives this week. Fox Weather reports that an atmospheric river reaches the California coast Tuesday night and puts more than 30 million people under a flash flood threat through Thursday. NOAA's Weather Prediction Center has a Level 2 flash flood risk for the Bay Area on Wednesday, with 2 to 3 inches of rain possible, and a Level 2 risk for the Los Angeles metro area on Thursday, when areas near wildfire burn scars face the added risk of debris flows.

SFist, citing a San Francisco Chronicle forecast, says much of the Bay Area could see 3 inches or more over the two days and the Santa Cruz Mountains 5 inches or more, with localized flooding likely in known trouble spots. The Weather Channel says 3 to 6 inches are possible along the California coastline and more than 6 inches in the foothills, and notes that soils have little time to dry between storms.

The short answer

We track 50 California cities with about 14.5 million residents. Thirty of them, home to about 11.1 million people, sit in a county that FEMA's National Risk Index rates Very High for riverine flooding (rivers and streams overflowing their banks). Another 19 rate Relatively High. The only one lower is Chico, at Relatively Moderate.

That is unusual nationally. Across the 299 U.S. cities we track with a rating, 58 are in Very High river flood counties, and 30 of the 58 are in California. Texas is next with 8, then Arizona with 6. The nine California counties at Very High are Los Angeles, Orange, San Diego, Riverside, San Bernardino, Ventura, Santa Clara, Alameda and Contra Costa.

Low annual rainfall does not mean low flood risk. Of the 49 California cities with a NOAA rainfall normal, 45 get less than 20 inches in a normal year, so a single storm can carry a large share of it.

Coastal flooding is a separate rating. Fifteen of the 50 California cities are in counties rated Relatively High for it (Los Angeles, Orange, Alameda, Santa Clara and San Mateo), and none rates Very High. For 21 inland cities, such as Fresno and Riverside, FEMA marks coastal flooding as not applicable.

What this means for a home search

  • Look up the official flood map for the address. FEMA's Flood Map Service Center shows whether the lot is in a mapped flood zone. Our guide on how to check flood risk before buying a house covers how to read the result.
  • Do not stop at the flood zone line. FloodSmart, the federal flood insurance program's site, says almost one-third of its flood claims come from outside high-risk flood areas, and that most homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. Get a flood insurance quote before you close, not after the first storm.
  • Visit during or right after rain if you can. Watch where water runs on the street, whether it pools at the driveway, and whether nearby storm drains keep up. Ask the seller and neighbors about past flooding.
  • Look uphill. In Los Angeles County, the Thursday forecast singles out areas near burn scars for debris flows. The flood ratings in this post do not measure that, so check whether a home sits below a recently burned slope.
  • Check the lowest level. Garages, basements and below-grade units flood first. Look for water lines, fresh paint low on walls, a sump pump or a backflow valve, and ask why they were added.

How we measured it

Flood ratings come from FEMA's National Risk Index for the county each city sits in. FEMA describes these ratings as relative risk compared with the rest of the United States, built from exposure, how often floods occur and historic losses. A county rating covers a large area, so a single home can be much safer or much riskier than its county. The address check on our site shows the rating for the census tract instead.

Rainfall is the NOAA 1991–2020 annual normal at the nearest station with complete records. Some cities use a station several miles away: Pomona uses Chino Airport, Richmond uses downtown San Francisco and Antioch uses Concord. The "3 in. =" column is simple division, 3 inches over the annual normal. Forecast figures come from the news reports listed below and may change as the storm develops.
