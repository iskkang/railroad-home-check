St. Louis train horns: the quiet zone is coming back. Here is what the federal records show

After about two months of horns at every crossing, St. Louis says routine train horns will stop again by mid-November. We matched the news against the federal crossing inventory: which lines are affected, how many trains run at night, and why a quiet-zone flag on paper is not a guarantee.

By the Home Surroundings Check editorial team · Drafted with AI assistance; figures checked against federal records

What happened

St. Louis had been a railroad quiet zone since 2005. In September 2026 the Federal Railroad Administration revoked that status because warning signs at crossings no longer met the rules, so train crews went back to sounding horns at every public crossing, day and night. Coverage named the Hill neighborhood, near Sublette and Manchester avenues, where the tracks run close to homes.

The city finished the required fixes in late October, including larger crossing signs and repainted stop bars, and the FRA agreed the quiet zone can be reinstated. On November 6 the city said horns should stop within seven to ten days, once railroad operating instructions are updated. Mayor Cara Spencer called the round-the-clock horns truly a disruption to our city (St. Louis Public Radio).

What the federal crossing records show

The FRA inventory lists 147 open public at-grade crossings for St. Louis (including records filed as "near" the city). Of those:

  • 138 are flagged as a 24-hour quiet zone.
  • 107 report a daily through-train count, and 99 report trains at night (6 PM to 6 AM).
  • At crossings with trains, the median is 6 trains a day; the busiest report 24.
  • Union Pacific (41 crossings), the Terminal Railroad Association of St. Louis (40) and Norfolk Southern (21) operate most of them.

Twelve night trains through six crossings meant a horn sequence (long, long, short, long) at each crossing for every train, which is why residents near this stretch felt the change so sharply.

Our take: a quiet-zone flag is a status, not a promise

The most useful detail for buyers is in the record itself. The inventory revision dated November 2, 2026, in the middle of the suspension, still lists these crossings as a 24-hour quiet zone. Anyone who checked the federal data in October would have seen "quiet zone" while horns were sounding outside.

Quiet zones depend on equipment and signs that the city must keep in order, and the FRA can suspend them. That makes three checks worth doing before an offer near tracks:

  1. Ask the city whether the quiet zone is currently in effect and when it was last reviewed.
  2. Visit after 10 PM. Quiet zones remove routine horns, not trains: you still hear the train itself, crossing bells and emergency horns.
  3. Look at night counts, not just the flag. On this Union Pacific line, half the trains run at night.
