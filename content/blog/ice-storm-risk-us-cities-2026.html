The short answer

Of the 300 cities in our train-activity pages, 57 sit in a county that FEMA's National Risk Index rates Very High for ice storm risk. They cover 42 counties and about 16.9 million people (2023 Census estimates). Another 75 cities rate Relatively High.

The list is not a cold-weather list. Arkansas has all six of the cities we track in Very High counties, and Oklahoma all five. Texas has 18. Meanwhile Denver, Anchorage and Idaho Falls, which each average more than 150 freezing nights a year, rate Very Low.

Ice matters for a house in a specific way. The National Weather Service says Ice Storm Warnings are "usually issued for ice accumulation of around 1/4 inch or more," an amount that makes travel dangerous and likely snaps power lines and drops tree branches. Its Baltimore/Washington office adds that the weight of ice can bring trees and lines down onto homes, and that major events can cause widespread outages lasting several days.

FEMA rates ice storms and general winter weather separately, and the two do not always line up. Of the 57 Very High ice storm cities, only 19 also rate Very High for winter weather.

Where it rarely freezes, ice still rates high

Twenty-one of the 57 cities average fewer than 40 freezing nights a year. Dallas averages 23 and about 1.7 inches of snow. Fort Worth averages 30, Shreveport 30 and Jackson, Mississippi 32. McAllen, in Hidalgo County, averages less than one freezing night a year and still rates Very High.

That last one is a reminder of what the rating measures. FEMA starts from expected annual loss, the average dollar loss from a hazard each year, then raises it for social vulnerability and lowers it for community resilience. The result is ranked against every other county. So a county with many people, a lot of building value or high social vulnerability can rate high even if ice is uncommon. Treat the rating as a sign of how costly ice tends to be there, not as a count of storms.

At the cold end, the picture flips. Of the 23 cities with 150 or more freezing nights a year, only three rate Very High for ice storms: Bismarck, Fargo and Sioux Falls. Twelve rate Relatively Low or Very Low. And 73 cities, all in California, Florida and Arizona, have no ice storm rating at all; FEMA marks them "Not Applicable."

What this means for a home search

Ice storm risk is easy to overlook in a city with mild winters, because most of the year gives no hint of it. On a showing or inspection:

  1. Look up. Note large trees or limbs over the roof, the driveway and the line that brings power to the house. The NWS warns that ice-loaded trees and lines can fall onto homes.
  2. Find the power line. See whether service arrives overhead or underground, and how far it runs through trees.
  3. Plan for days without power. Ask how the house is heated and whether that heat needs electricity to run. Outages after a major ice storm can last several days.
  4. Read the disclosures for storm history. Roof, gutter or tree-damage repairs and past insurance claims can point to an earlier ice event.
  5. Check the pipes too. The same cold snap can freeze plumbing; our frozen-pipe guide covers what to ask.
  6. Check the address, not just the city. Our address report shows the census-tract National Risk Index ratings for a specific home, alongside rail, road and flood data.

How we measured it

Ice storm and winter weather ratings are FEMA National Risk Index county ratings for the county at each city's center point. Ratings run from Very Low to Very High and compare each county with all other counties. Freezing nights and snowfall are NOAA 1991–2020 climate normals from the nearest station with complete temperature records; snowfall is not recorded at many of those stations, so we did not rank by it. Populations are Census Vintage 2023 estimates. Limits: a county rating covers the whole county, so suburbs, rural edges and the urban core all share one value; the city-to-county match uses one point; and none of these figures predict what a given winter will bring.
