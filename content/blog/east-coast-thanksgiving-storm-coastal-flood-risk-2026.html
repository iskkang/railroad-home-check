What happened

Charleston faced a major flood forecast on the day before Thanksgiving. The Post and Courier reported that the city's tide tracker called for a high tide of about 8.4 feet at the Charleston Harbor gauge around 8 a.m. on Wednesday, Nov. 25, the highest tide forecast for the city this year, and that the National Weather Service issued a coastal flood warning for that morning. By the Weather Service's thresholds cited in the article, 7 feet is minor flooding, 7.5 feet moderate, and 8 feet or more major. At that level, parts of the downtown peninsula, including Market Street, East Bay, Rutledge and the Medical District, flood and some roads become impassable.

The same storm then moves north. The Weather Channel says moderate to major coastal flooding is possible from Georgia and the Carolinas to Long Island, with 1 to 4 inches of rain over much of the East and gusts above 50 mph from the Virginia Tidewater to New England. Fox Weather says the Delaware Bay and southern New Jersey may see their highest water levels since Superstorm Sandy at Thursday morning's high tide, and lists High Wind Watches for Maryland, New Jersey, eastern Pennsylvania, Long Island, New York City and Philadelphia. ABC News says roads in Atlantic City could be under water around the 8 a.m. high tide on Thursday.

The short answer

Coastal flood risk is concentrated in a small number of places. FEMA's National Risk Index defines coastal flooding as water covering normally dry coastal land because of high or rising tides or storm surge. Of the 299 cities we track that have a FEMA rating, 195 are inland and FEMA marks coastal flooding as not applicable for their county.

Only 10 sit in a county rated Very High. Seven are in Florida. The other three are on this week's storm path: Charleston, North Charleston and Savannah. All 10 are also rated Very High for hurricane risk.

Further north, the highest ratings are Relatively High, and they are not limited to beach towns. Philadelphia and Wilmington, Delaware, both on the Delaware River, rate Relatively High, as do Norfolk, Wilmington, North Carolina, and Brooklyn (Kings County), the county we use for New York City.

Half of the 30 Relatively High cities are in California, mostly in Los Angeles, Orange and Alameda counties, which we covered in our post on California's atmospheric river. Florida has five more, including Jacksonville and Tampa, and the Gulf Coast adds Houston, Baytown, Mobile, Gulfport and Lake Charles.

The Charleston numbers show why a rating is only a starting point. The Post and Courier, citing the Weather Service, counts 48 tidal flood events in Charleston this year through the end of October, 32 of them minor. It also cites the city's Water Plan, which says more than 70 percent of recorded major tidal floods there have happened since 2015.

What this means for a home search

  • Look up the flood map for the address, not the city. FEMA's Flood Map Service Center shows whether the lot is in a Special Flood Hazard Area. FEMA says homes there have at least a 26% chance of flooding over a 30-year mortgage. Our guide on how to check flood risk before buying a house explains how to read it.
  • Check the streets, not just the house. In Charleston, a major tide makes some downtown roads impassable, so a dry house can still be cut off. Drive the route to work, school and the nearest hospital and ask neighbors which streets flood at high tide.
  • Visit at a king tide or right after a storm. Tide tables are published well ahead. Look for standing salt water in gutters, storm drains backing up, and water lines on garage doors, fences and walls.
  • Look at elevation and the lowest level. Ask for an elevation certificate if one exists. Ground-floor garages, crawl spaces and HVAC units on the ground are the first to take water.
  • Get a flood insurance quote before you close. A high-tide nuisance flood and a storm surge are different events, but both can be costly for a home on low ground. Price the coverage while you can still walk away.

How we measured it

Ratings come from FEMA's National Risk Index for the county each city sits in. FEMA's coastal flooding page lists what goes into them: expected annual losses to buildings and people, the building value and population exposed, how often floods are modeled to occur, and historic losses. The ratings compare each county with the rest of the United States, so a county with more people and property can rate higher than a smaller one with similar tides. A county rating covers a large area: inland parts of Los Angeles County, such as Palmdale, carry the same coastal rating as Long Beach. For New York City we use Kings County (Brooklyn), so the rating does not describe all five boroughs. The address check on our site shows the census-tract rating instead.
