Homes in these cities are generally built for it: pipes inside heated space, deeper water lines, basements. Freezing is expected, so the questions are about maintenance, not design.

Where freezes are rare, and that is the problem

Of the 300 cities, 43 average between 1 and 12 freezing nights a year and another 31 average fewer than one. Rare does not mean harmless. The American Red Cross notes that frozen pipes are a particular problem in warmer climates, where water lines often run through uninsulated attics and crawl spaces. A house built for heat can have its plumbing in exactly the places a hard freeze reaches first.

FEMA's National Risk Index shows the same pattern. Several warm-weather counties rate high for cold waves, because when one does arrive, the losses are large:

FEMA ratings measure expected losses compared with other counties, so they rise with population and property value as well as weather. They are a signal to look closer, not a forecast for a particular house.

Our take: in a warm city, ask where the pipes run

In a cold city, a buyer already expects winterized plumbing. In a city with four freezing nights a year, nobody asks, which is why the questions matter more there. Before an offer, or before the first cold snap in a new home:

  1. Find the pipes. Ask the inspector which supply lines run through the attic, garage, crawl space or exterior walls, and whether they are insulated.
  2. Look for past freeze repairs. Patched ceilings, a short run of newer pipe in an older system, or water-damage claims in the seller's disclosure can all point to an earlier freeze.
  3. Know the shutoff. Locate the main water shutoff and outdoor hose-bib valves on the walkthrough; in a burst, minutes matter.
  4. Check the address, not just the city. Our report shows the FEMA National Risk Index for the census tract of a specific home, alongside its rail, road and flood picture.
