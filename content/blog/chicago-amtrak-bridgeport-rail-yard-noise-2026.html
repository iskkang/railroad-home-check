What happened

On November 6, the Federal Railroad Administration signed a draft environmental assessment for Amtrak's planned Midwest Maintenance Facility. The project would turn the Union Pacific-owned Canal Street Yard in Bridgeport into the place where Amtrak inspects and services its Chicago trains, moving that work from the 14th Street Yard just south of Union Station, about 1.8 miles away. Chicago City Wire reports that the site covers about 54 acres between Canal Street and Stewart Avenue, from just north of Cermak Road to 38th Street. The plan also repairs 17 viaducts and bridges along the yard, and construction is scheduled from January 2027 through August 2028.

The draft rates the effects "insignificant" in every category it summarizes, noise included. It is not a final decision. After the public inspection period, FRA will either issue a Finding of No Significant Impact or prepare a full Environmental Impact Statement. Ald. Nicole Lee, whose 11th Ward includes the area, called the assessment "woefully insufficient," Sox Machine reports, and said nearly 7,000 residents live within 500 feet of the yard.

What the noise study found

The noise and vibration study, prepared for FRA by the firm WSP, assumed about 29 train movements a day in the yard. It predicted yard noise of 46 to 65 dBA at the homes it modeled and found no severe impacts. Four clusters of homes fall in the moderate range, which under the Federal Transit Administration criteria the study used does not count as significant. RaillyNews reports the same four clusters.

Levels are dBA Ldn, a day-night average that counts night noise more heavily. "Now" is the existing level and "Yard" the predicted noise from yard operations alone, as reported by Chicago City Wire. At R-33 the yard alone would be louder than what residents hear today. At the other three it would be 1 to 2 dB below today's level, which is already high.

Most of the added noise comes from wheels crossing turnouts and crossovers, about 5 dB more at the frogs, where one rail crosses another. Predicted vibration at the nearest homes is 43 to 70 VdB, below the FTA's 80 VdB criterion, although homes west of the south yard would feel new daily vibration events.

The study says a continuous noise barrier along the yard's west edge would bring all four clusters down to no impact, with movable-point frogs as an alternative. Amtrak says it will weigh a barrier, frogs or both during final design; the documents do not commit to either. On its project site, Amtrak describes the yard as "primarily a daytime operation, with limited overnight work."

How loud Chicago's rails already are

USDOT's National Transportation Noise Map gives a citywide baseline. Matched to 2020 census population, 71.7% of Chicago residents live where modeled rail noise averages 45 dB or more over 24 hours, and 7.1%, about 195,000 people, where it reaches 55 dB or more. That 55 dB share is the highest of the 33 cities we track with at least 500,000 residents. Among all 299 cities, only Hammond, Indiana and New Braunfels, Texas rank higher. In Chicago, rail at 55 dB or more reaches more residents than road traffic (4.2%) or aircraft (3.6%).

Crossings add their own noise. The FRA inventory lists 303 public crossings in or near Chicago, 241 with trains reported at night, and 133 in quiet zones, 118 of them under Chicago's own horn exemption. Our Chicago page maps them.

What it means for a home search

  1. Note which side of the yard. The proposed barrier would run along the yard's west edge, and the cluster near Canal and 30th sits west of the north yard. Near the Canal Street–Stewart Avenue corridor between Cermak and 38th, check the distance and line of sight from the house to the tracks.
  2. Plan for construction. Work is scheduled from January 2027 to August 2028. City rules allow construction from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m., and Chicago City Wire reports that some viaduct work may need nights or weekends. Sox Machine reports truck routes on 26th, 31st and 33rd streets.
  3. Ask what is final. A noise barrier is under evaluation, not promised. Before closing, check whether FRA has issued its decision and whether mitigation is part of it.
  4. Visit at the right hours. Amtrak says trains will arrive mostly mid-to-late morning and leave in the afternoon or early evening. Yard noise sounds different from horns: wheels on switches, idling, coupling. Our night train noise guide and vibration checklist cover what to listen and feel for.
  5. Check the address. Our address report shows modeled rail, road and aircraft noise at a specific home, plus the nearest crossings.
  6. Weigh in if you live nearby. FRA lists an in-person forum on December 2, 5 to 8 p.m., at Illinois Institute of Technology's Hermann Hall (3241 S. Federal St.) and virtual sessions on December 3. Written comments go through Regulations.gov, docket FRA-2026-2938, through December 7.

City size uses Census Vintage 2023 estimates. Crossing counts come from the FRA crossing inventory as retrieved on November 9, 2026. Limits: a citywide share says nothing about a single block; the map is a model, not a measurement; and it reflects 2020 traffic, before this yard plan.
