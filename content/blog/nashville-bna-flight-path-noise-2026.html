What happened

Last week Nashville's Metro Council approved a substitute resolution, 25 to 2 with one abstention, backing residents of southern Davidson County who say departing jets from Nashville International Airport (BNA) now pass over their homes far more often. The resolution, sponsored by Councilmember Mike Cortese, asks the Metropolitan Nashville Airport Authority to keep relaying residents' concerns to the FAA, make aircraft and noise information public, and notify residents of future FAA proposals that significantly change departure procedures. As quoted by NewsChannel 5, it cites residents reporting more than 150 flights over some homes on certain days and noise as high as 90 decibels. One resident told the station: "It's five a.m. till midnight some days."

The resolution asks; it does not order. As WKRN noted, it does not mean flight paths will move from one district to another (WKRN).

The dispute is over FAA departure-heading changes that took effect in November 2025 and were amended in July 2026. In September, the City of Oak Hill, the group Course Correct Nashville, the Crieve Hall Neighborhood Association and resident Kelly Insana asked the Sixth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to review them. They argue the changes concentrated traffic over Oak Hill and Radnor Lake State Park instead of spreading it out, and that the FAA skipped proper community engagement (WSMV). The FAA does not comment on pending litigation. It says it presented the new headings at a virtual public workshop in March 2025 and that they improve separation between aircraft and get departures on their way more efficiently (FAA). In a follow-up report, one council member said constituents recently reported fewer, quieter flights; another said that may reflect seasonal changes in flight patterns (WKRN).

Two things stand out. Aircraft noise is the most widespread source in Nashville: about one resident in five lives where it averages 45 dB or more, three times the share for roads. But it is rarely loud on average. At 55 dB or more, roads reach more than three times as many residents as aircraft.

Why the map can't settle this dispute

The noise map uses 2020 flight data, five years before the November 2025 heading changes. It cannot show who gained or lost overflights since then. And a 24-hour average smooths out exactly what residents describe: a jet every few minutes, each one far louder than the average while it passes. A street can sit in a modest band and still hear departures from early morning to midnight.

What this means for a home search near BNA

  1. Check the address, then go stand there. Our address report shows modeled aircraft, road and rail noise at the home separately. Treat it as a baseline, then visit early in the morning and again late in the evening, the hours residents complain about.
  2. Visit more than once. Council members disagree on whether recent relief is real or seasonal. One quiet afternoon proves little.
  3. Ask the seller and neighbors directly. Ask whether overflights changed after November 2025, and check whether the airport authority has published the aircraft and noise information the council asked for.
  4. Watch the court case. The Sixth Circuit petition could change routes, or confirm them. Until it is resolved, today's flight pattern is not guaranteed to be next year's.
  5. Don't forget the ground. At 55 dB or more, roads reach more Nashville residents than aircraft and rail combined. Our Nashville train page covers the city's rail crossings and night trains.

How we measured it

We read the USDOT Bureau of Transportation Statistics National Transportation Noise Map (2020 data, 24-hour equivalent sound level) at the population center of every 2020 census block group inside each city's limits, and weighted by population. Nashville here means the Nashville-Davidson metropolitan government area; Oak Hill, a separate city named in the lawsuit, is not included. The same method is behind our ranking of the loudest U.S. cities. The map is a coarse national model, and a block group's center stands in for everyone who lives there. News details come from the reports linked above; flight counts and decibel levels in the first sections are residents' reports, not measurements by us.
