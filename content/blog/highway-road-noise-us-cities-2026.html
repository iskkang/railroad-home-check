What happened

AAA projected 81.5 million Americans would travel at least 50 miles from home between Tuesday, Nov. 24 and Monday, Nov. 30, Fox Weather reported. Of those, 72.9 million were expected to go by car, nearly 90% of all travelers and slightly fewer than last year's 73.1 million.

The holiday rush ends. The highways it used do not. For homes next to a freeway or a busy arterial, traffic noise is a daily condition, and it is one of the few surroundings a buyer can't change after closing. So we asked a narrower question than in our ranking of the loudest cities overall: leaving out trains and planes, where do the most residents live with loud road traffic?

The short answer

Across the 299 cities we track, about 2.0 million of 81.9 million residents, roughly 2.5%, live where the federal model puts road noise at a 24-hour average of 55 dB or more outdoors. About 770,000 live at 60 dB or more.

The typical city is quieter than that total suggests. In the median city, 1.6% of residents are in 55 dB road areas. Twenty-five cities reach 5% or more. In 70 cities, not one neighborhood's population center lands in a 55 dB road area.

Roads are still the most common source of loud exposure. Of the 270 cities with any residents at 55 dB or more from all transportation combined, roads are the largest single source in 172; rail leads in 41 and aircraft in 39, and 18 are ties.

Why 55 dB: in 1974 EPA identified 55 dB outdoors (and 45 dB indoors) as the level that prevents activity interference and annoyance. BTS compares the 55–59 dB band to a restaurant and 60–69 dB to a vacuum cleaner. These are full-day averages, so the loudest moments of the day run higher than the number shown.

South Gate stands out at every level: it also has 2.5% of residents at 70 dB or more from roads. Kissimmee is the opposite case. Its 11.0% all sit in the 55–60 dB band, with none above 60.

Most of the list is mid-size cities. When a city has only a few dozen census block groups, a handful of loud ones can move its share several points. Seattle is the exception: at 8.1%, about 60,000 residents, it has the highest share among the 33 cities with 500,000 or more people.

What this means for a home search

  • Check the address, not the city. A low city share does not make a house beside a freeway quiet. Our noise guide covers road traffic, night trains and airports around an address, and the road traffic check shows reported daily vehicle counts nearby.
  • Look for a wall, and for gaps in it. BTS says the model does not account for barriers or terrain. A home behind a solid noise wall may be quieter than the map shows; one at the end of a wall, or across from an on-ramp, may not be.
  • Visit at rush hour and late at night. A 24-hour average hides the peaks. Listen outside, then in the bedrooms with the windows shut. Our busy-road guide lists what to look for.
  • Ask about widening plans. Under federal rules, adding a through lane or building a highway on a new route is a "Type I" project, and the agency must analyze traffic noise for each alternative. A home counts as impacted if its predicted loudest-hour level outdoors comes within 1 dB of 67 dB(A), or rises substantially above today's level. That hourly standard is not directly comparable to the 24-hour averages above.
  • Weigh road and rail together. Some blocks sit near both a highway and a rail line. Our road and rail comparison lets you compare up to three addresses side by side.

How we measured it

We used the road layer of USDOT's National Transportation Noise Map, which models noise as a 24-hour average (LAeq) from 2020 data. For each city we took the 2020 census city limits, placed every census block group inside them at its population center, read the modeled road noise band there, and weighted by 2020 population. All-source figures come from the same method used in our overall ranking. Savannah, Georgia is left out because its center point fell outside the city limits.

Limits: BTS says the data should not be used to evaluate noise at individual locations or specific times, and it does not count shielding from barriers or terrain. One population center stands in for a whole block group, so a city can show 0% and still have homes right next to a highway. City limits follow the census place: Louisville's figures cover the place the Census Bureau lists as "Louisville city" (about 246,000 residents in 2020), not the full consolidated metro government. The AAA figures are projections for this year's holiday, not measured counts.
