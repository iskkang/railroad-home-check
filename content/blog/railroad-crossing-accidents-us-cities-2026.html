What happened

On the afternoon of November 11, a BNSF train struck a tanker truck carrying beef tallow at a railroad crossing in the 600 block of NE 29th Street in Fort Worth, FOX 4 reports. The truck appears to have become stuck on the tracks while trying to cross. Video obtained by the station shows the driver jumping out and running clear seconds before the collision. BNSF said one of its employees was taken to a hospital with non-life-threatening injuries, and that it is working with local police on the investigation. In its statement, the railroad said, "Drivers and pedestrians must stay clear of railroad tracks at all times." No official cause has been reported.

Crashes like this are not rare. Operation Lifesaver, citing Federal Railroad Administration data, lists 2,274 collisions at public and private crossings nationwide in 2025, with 284 deaths and 773 injuries (preliminary figures). Texas had the most of any state, 262, followed by California (181) and Florida (142).

The short answer

The 300 cities we track have 16,847 open public at-grade crossings. Between January 2021 and August 2026, the FRA received 2,894 highway-rail incident reports at those crossings. The reports involve 1,938 crossings, about 11.5% of the total. Only 27 of the 300 cities had no report at all.

Texas stands out here too. Its 42 cities on our list account for 528 reports, about 18% of the total, more than any other state. California cities follow with 463 and Florida cities with 246. Fort Worth logged 41 reports at 31 of its 177 public crossings, which ranks ninth by count. The November 11 crash is too recent to appear in the data, which runs through August 31, 2026.

Reports often repeat at the same crossing. In Midland, all 42 reports came from 11 crossings. In Phoenix, 51 reports came from 13 of its 102 crossings, and in Oakland 40 came from 17 crossings. Across all 300 cities, the 1,938 crossings with any report averaged about 1.5 reports each. The records do not explain why some crossings repeat, and we do not guess at causes here.

What this means for a home search

  • Find the crossings you will use, not just the nearest one. A crossing on the way to school, work or the grocery store matters even if the house is several blocks from the tracks. Our guide on how to check crossing accident history explains how crossing IDs link to incident records.
  • Look for repeats. One report in nearly six years says little. Several reports at the same crossing are worth a closer look, and worth asking the city about planned changes.
  • See what protects the crossing. Gates, flashing lights or only a crossbuck sign tell you what warning a driver or walker gets. About 53% of the crossings in our 300 cities report at least one roadway gate arm; in Fort Worth it is 138 of 177.
  • Watch how traffic backs up. The Fort Worth truck was caught on the tracks. Visit at rush hour and check whether cars queue across the rails when a signal or intersection sits just past the crossing.
  • Check the noise side too. Each city page shows day and night train counts and quiet-zone status, and our post on night trains outside quiet zones ranks where horns are most likely after dark.

How we measured it

Crossings come from the FRA's crossing inventory (Form 71): open, public, at-grade crossings whose FRA city code matches one of our 300 cities, including records listed as near the city. Incidents are FRA highway-rail accident/incident reports (Form 57) dated January 1, 2021 through August 31, 2026, matched to those crossings by crossing ID. They include collisions involving vehicles and pedestrians. Reports at private crossings, closed crossings or without a crossing ID are not counted, so our city totals cover only part of each state's crashes. "Per 100" is reports divided by public crossings, times 100.

A report count is history, not a forecast. The counts do not adjust for how many trains or cars use each crossing. Warning equipment can be upgraded and crossings can be closed after the reports were filed. National and state figures come from Operation Lifesaver's summaries of FRA data, and the 2025 numbers are preliminary.
