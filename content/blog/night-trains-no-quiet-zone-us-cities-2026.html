The short answer

Across the 300 cities on our train activity pages, the federal inventory lists 16,847 open public at-grade crossings. 9,309 of them, about 55%, report at least one through train at night. The FRA form defines night as 6 PM to 6 AM.

Most of those night crossings have no quiet zone. On our city maps, at least 7,606 crossings report night trains and are not flagged as a quiet zone, more than four in five of all night-train crossings. Federal rules in 49 CFR Part 222 set standards for sounding locomotive horns as trains approach and pass through public crossings, and a quiet zone is where routine horn use is restricted. 174 of the 300 cities report no quiet-zone crossing at all.

Houston leads by a wide margin, with 240 night-train crossings outside a quiet zone. San Antonio, Baton Rouge, Fort Worth and Memphis follow among cities where railroad-company lines carry most of the traffic.

Two rows need a caution. In Dallas, only 58 of the 160 crossings with night trains are on railroad-company lines; the rest are on transit or commuter lines, and DART alone is listed on 93 crossings. In San Diego the figure is 6 of 100, with the San Diego Trolley the most common operator. Before reading a night count there as freight traffic, check on the city page which line actually runs near the home.

Baton Rouge and Jacksonville stand out the other way: every night-train crossing there is on a railroad-company line, and neither city reports a single quiet-zone crossing.

"+3" means three more crossings in the same city report the same night count, usually because they sit on one stretch of track. In Kansas City, Missouri, Mulberry, St. Louis Avenue, Hickory and Union Avenue each list 50 Union Pacific trains a night and none is in a quiet zone. Auburn's A Street, a few miles south of Kent's new quiet zone, reports 67 by night and 67 by day.

Three of the eight are in 24-hour quiet zones. That limits routine horn use at those crossings; it does not change the number of trains or the rumble of each one. Our quiet-zone guide covers what the status does and does not promise, and the St. Louis case shows that it can be revoked.

What this means for a home search

  1. Find the nearest crossing, not the city average. Our address check shows the closest public crossing with its FRA ID, day and night counts and quiet-zone flag.
  2. Read the count year. The form calls these "estimated" daily train movements and records the year they were counted. Auburn's A Street, Amarillo and Kansas City, Kansas, list 2023 counts; Gary and South Bend list 2026.
  3. Look beyond through trains. The form counts switching moves separately from the through trains in these tables. Near a yard or industrial spur, ask about switching at night.
  4. Check every crossing within earshot. A horn sounds on the approach to each crossing, so a home between two unprotected crossings can hear both. Our guide to train horns near a house explains why the nearest crossing can matter more than distance to the tracks.
  5. Visit after 10 PM. Listen outside, then inside with the windows shut. The night freight noise guide has a checklist.

How we measured it

We used the FRA Highway-Rail Crossing Inventory as retrieved on November 9, 2026: open, public, at-grade crossings matched to each city by the inventory's city code, which includes records filed as "near" the city. A crossing counts as a night-train crossing if it reports at least one through train between 6 PM and 6 AM. A crossing is "in a quiet zone" if the inventory flags it as 24-hour, partial or Chicago Excused. The first table uses the categories on our city crossing maps; a small number of crossings that fall outside a city's map frame (five each in Jacksonville and San Diego) are not classified, so their counts are a floor. The second table uses each city's busiest railroad-company crossings, excluding transit and commuter agencies.

Limits: train counts are estimates, many records list identical day and night figures (as in Auburn and Kansas City), and some counts are years old. A quiet-zone flag reflects the record, which can lag behind a new or revoked zone. None of this measures sound at a particular house; distance, terrain, buildings and windows all matter.
